# Sound Symbolism Reference

This reference is based on research by David Placek and Lexicon Branding (a four-year
study across nine countries on phonetic associations), academic sound symbolism
research (Sapir 1929, Ohala 1994, Argo 2010), and applied naming linguistics.

## Core Principle

Every letter and sound in the English alphabet evokes specific psychological
associations.
These associations are often cross-cultural -- Lexicon's research
found many phonetic signals work globally, not just in English.
When building
a name, select sounds that reinforce the brand's desired traits from the
Creative Framework.

## Consonant Associations

### High-Energy / Vitality Sounds
- **V** -- The most alive and vibrant sound.
  Signals movement, vitality, daring.
  Cross-culturally reliable.
  Examples: Vercel, Viagra, Corvette, Visa, Verizon.
  Use when the brand should feel dynamic, forward-moving, or bold.

- **Z** -- Signals speed, electricity, edge.
  Has a buzzing, energetic quality.
  Examples: Zappos, Zoom, Zendesk, Azure.
  Use for tech brands that want to
  feel fast or innovative.

### Reliability / Stability Sounds
- **B** -- Signals reliability, solidity, and stability.
  Has a grounded, sturdy
  quality.
  Examples: BlackBerry, Boeing, BMW.
  The explosive initial "B" feels
  energetic but dependable.

- **M** -- Warm, comforting, approachable.
  Associated with nurturing and
  satisfaction (possibly linked to "mmm" as a satisfaction sound).
  Examples:
  Marriott, Mercedes, Microsoft.

- **N** -- Gentle, sonorous, comforting.
  Similar warmth to M but slightly more
  neutral.
  Examples: Nike, Netflix, Nintendo.

### Precision / Innovation Sounds
- **X** -- Denotes innovation, precision, and technological sophistication.
  Has a cutting, exact quality.
  Examples: Xerox, SpaceX, Xbox.
  Use for brands
  that want to signal cutting-edge technology.

- **K / hard C** -- Sharp, crisp, exact.
  Signals precision and decisiveness.
  Examples: Kodak, Kayak, Kindle.
  The hard stop creates a memorable "snap" in
  the ear.

### Plosive Sounds (P, K, T, B, D, G)
Plosives create a burst of air -- they signal crispness, exactness, and energy.
Names with plosives tend to feel more decisive and memorable.
The harder plosives
(P, K, T) feel more precise; the softer ones (B, D, G) feel more grounded.
Examples: Pentium (P+T), Kodak (K+K), BlackBerry (B+K+B).

### Fricative Sounds (F, S, Sh, Th)
Fricatives involve continuous airflow -- they signal smoothness, flow, and speed.
- **S / Sw** -- Speed, smoothness, effortlessness.
  Examples: Swiffer, Sonos, Safari.
  The "Sw" combination is particularly effective for suggesting fluid movement.
- **F** -- Lightness, air, freedom.
  Examples: Firefox, Febreze.
- **Sh** -- Softness, quiet, calm.
  Examples: Shazam (though here paired with
  a hard "zam" for contrast).

### Liquid Sounds (L, R)
- **L** -- Smooth, flowing, elegant.
  Signals luxury or ease.
  Examples: Lexus,
  Lucid, Lyft.
- **R** -- Energy, movement, rolling motion.
  The tongue is most agitated
  pronouncing R, which makes it effective for conveying action.
  Examples:
  Range Rover, Roku.

## Vowel Architecture

Vowels create the "body" of a name.
Their sound quality shapes how expansive,
precise, or complete a name feels.

- **A (open "ah")** -- Expansive, open, grand.
  Creates a sense of scale and
  possibility.
  Examples: Amazon, Asana, Azure.
  Use for brands that want to
  feel large or encompassing.

- **O (round "oh")** -- Holistic, complete, whole.
  Creates a sense of
  containment and satisfaction.
  Examples: Sonos, Oracle, Roku.
  Particularly
  effective in palindromes.

- **I (sharp "ee")** -- Precise, small, focused.
  Creates a sense of exactness
  and specificity.
  Examples: Siri, Wii, Fiji.
  Academic research (Sapir 1929)
  consistently links front high vowels to perceptions of smallness and precision.

- **E (open "eh")** -- Neutral, accessible, friendly.
  The most "transparent"
  vowel -- it doesn't add strong coloring.
  Examples: Tesla, Reddit, Etsy.

- **U (round "oo")** -- Depth, power, weight.
  Creates a sense of substance
  and gravity.
  Examples: Uber, Ubuntu, Lucid.

## Compound Name Mechanics

Compound names (two words or morphemes combined) act as meaning multipliers.
The combination creates a meaning greater than the sum of its parts (1+1=3).

**Effective compound patterns:**
- **Action + Object:** PowerBook, Windsurf, Airbnb.
  The action word energizes
  the object word.
- **Quality + Domain:** BlackBerry, Wholefoods, Snapchat.
  The quality word
  reframes the domain word.
- **Nature + Tech:** Snowflake, Cloudflare, Firebolt.
  Natural imagery makes
  abstract tech feel tangible and intuitive.

**Compound name principles:**
- Both components should be simple, common words (≤2 syllables each)
- The combination should create surprise -- if both words are from the same
  domain, the compound feels predictable
- Test the compound by saying it aloud rapidly 5 times -- it should flow
  naturally and not create awkward consonant clusters

## Alliteration and Repetition

Sound repetition (alliteration, assonance, consonance) significantly increases
memorability and positive evaluation.
Research by Argo (2010) found that names
with phonetic repetition were evaluated more favorably, especially when spoken
aloud.

- **Alliteration:** BlackBerry, Coca-Cola, PayPal, TikTok.
  The repeated
  initial sound creates a rhythmic "hook" in memory.
- **Assonance (vowel repetition):** Sonos, Kayak.
  Repeated vowel sounds
  create internal music.
- **Palindromes:** Sonos, Kayak.
  These read the same forwards and backwards,
  creating visual symmetry that reinforces memorability.
  Placek chose Sonos
  partly because it embodies the concept of sound that "works in every
  direction."

## Syllable Count Guidelines

- **1 syllable:** Maximum impact, feels bold and decisive (e.g., Slack, Stripe,
  Zoom).
  Hard to trademark without distinctive spelling.
- **2 syllables:** The sweet spot for most brand names.
  Easy to say, easy to
  remember, and distinctive enough to protect legally (e.g., Google, Apple,
  Sonos, Azure).
- **3 syllables:** Still effective if the phonetic rhythm is natural.
  Works
  well for names that need to feel more substantial or global (e.g., Pentium,
  Amazon, Corvette, Impossible).
- **4+ syllables:** Generally too long unless it's a compound name that breaks
  naturally into two clear parts (e.g., BlackBerry, PowerBook).

## Process Note

When generating names in Phase 2, don't try to optimize every name for phonetic
perfection.
Generate freely first, then use this reference to *analyze* and
*refine* the strongest candidates.
Sound symbolism is a filtering and polishing
tool, not a generation constraint.
