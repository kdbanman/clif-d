# Evaluation Filters Reference

## The Naming Taxonomy (Igor International / Zinzin)

This four-quadrant taxonomy helps classify names by their strategic approach.
Use it to map the competitive landscape and identify open territory.

### Functional Names
**What they do:** Describe what the company or product does, often literally.
May include the founder's name, a geographic reference, or a direct description
of the service.

**Examples:** E*Trade (electronic trading), Dropbox (a box for dropping files),
General Electric, Bank of America, LookSmart, GoToMeeting

**Strengths:** Immediately clear, low marketing cost to explain the product.

**Weaknesses:** Hard to defend legally (descriptive terms are difficult to
trademark), lack emotional resonance, easily commoditized, restrictive if the
company pivots.
These names do the least strategic work.

**When to use:** Only when the category is brand new and needs to be explained
to exist, or when the product is so simple that description *is* the value
proposition.
In most cases, avoid.

---

### Invented Names
**What they do:** Fabricated words with no prior dictionary meaning.
May be
built from Greek/Latin roots, phonetic construction, or pure invention.

**Examples:** Sonos, Pentium, Xerox, Kodak, Spotify, Häagen-Dazs, Accenture

**Strengths:** Highly trademarkable, no baggage or competing associations,
can be phonetically engineered for specific traits, signal "new and innovative."
Lexicon's research across six markets found that invented names like Pentium
actually take *less* money to build into brands than existing words (contrary to
conventional wisdom), because their novelty generates more attention.

**Weaknesses:** Require more initial marketing investment to imbue with meaning,
risk feeling cold or corporate if not phonetically warm, some can feel like
"alphabet soup" (e.g., Agilent, Accenture).

**When to use:** When you want to own a completely new word, when the competitive
landscape is cluttered with real-word names, or when you need global linguistic
clearance (invented words are less likely to mean something unfortunate in
another language).

---

### Experiential Names
**What they do:** Connect to direct human experience -- the act of using the
product, a sensory quality, or a real-world activity.
More imaginative than
Functional, but still literal.

**Examples:** Safari (browsing as exploration), Explorer (same metaphor),
Palm Pilot (guidance in your hand), Netscape (navigating a landscape)

**Strengths:** Intuitive, create immediate associations, help users understand
the product through analogy.

**Weaknesses:** Can feel dated if the experience metaphor becomes stale, often
clustered (many competitors in the same category choose similar experience
metaphors), moderately hard to trademark.

**When to use:** When the product experience is genuinely novel and the metaphor
is fresh, or when the audience needs a "bridge" concept to understand a new
category.

---

### Evocative Names
**What they do:** Use metaphor to represent the *soul* or positioning of the
company rather than describing the product, service, or direct experience.
They operate at a higher level of abstraction.

**Examples:** Virgin (rebellion, purity, doing things differently), Patagonia
(rugged wilderness, adventure, environmental consciousness), Amazon (vast,
unfathomably large), Apple (simplicity, accessibility, thinking differently),
Impossible (defying expectations)

**Strengths:** The strongest strategic play.
Hardest for competitors to copy,
easiest to trademark, most emotionally resonant, most "pivot-proof" (the name
doesn't tie you to a specific product), and they provide the richest material
for extended branding (stories, imagery, sub-brands).

**Weaknesses:** Require confidence and organizational buy-in (they feel risky
precisely because they *don't* describe the product), may initially confuse
literal-minded stakeholders.

**When to use:** Almost always -- unless there's a compelling reason to use
another type.
Evocative names are particularly powerful when entering a category
dominated by Functional or Invented names, because they immediately differentiate.

## SMILE Test -- Qualities of a Strong Name
*Framework by Alexandra Watkins, "Hello, My Name Is Awesome"*

Score each candidate on these five qualities.
A strong name should hit at
least 3 of 5 convincingly.

### S -- Suggestive
Does the name evoke something about the brand -- not in a descriptive way, but
in a metaphorical or associative way?

**Strong example:** Amazon -- suggests vastness, an enormous river, unfathomable
scale.
Jeff Bezos chose it because he envisioned his company being immeasurably
large, and the name never limited what they could sell.

**Weak example:** "Global Cleaning Solutions" -- describes the category but
suggests nothing distinctive about the brand.

**Test question:** "If you heard this name with no other context, what would
you *feel* or *imagine*?"

---

### M -- Memorable
Is the name based on a familiar word, concept, or phonetic pattern that makes
it easy to remember?
The brain retains the familiar more easily than the
completely alien.

**Strong example:** Swiffer -- built on "swift" (familiar concept of speed)
with a playful suffix.
You hear it once and it sticks.

**Weak example:** "Xobni" ("inbox" spelled backwards) -- clever in theory but
requires explanation to remember.

**Test question:** "If someone heard this name at a dinner party, could they
recall it the next morning?"

---

### I -- Imagery
Does the name conjure a visual image or sensory experience in the mind's eye?
Names that create pictures are dramatically more memorable than abstract ones.

**Strong example:** Patagonia -- instantly evokes rugged mountain landscapes,
glaciers, wind-swept wilderness.

**Weak example:** "Accenture" -- creates no visual image; it's an abstract
constructed word.

**Test question:** "Close your eyes and say this name.
What do you see?"

---

### L -- Legs
Does the name have a *theme* that can be extended across branding, marketing,
product lines, and community?
Can you build a world around it?

**Strong example:** Virgin -- the "virgin" territory metaphor extends everywhere:
Virgin Atlantic, Virgin Galactic, Virgin Records.
The name creates endless
possibilities for "first time" and "fresh start" messaging.

**Weak example:** "ProMop" -- there's nowhere to go with this.
You can't build
a brand world around a mop descriptor.

**Test question:** "Can you imagine a tagline, a community name, a sub-brand,
or a T-shirt slogan that plays off this name?"

---

### E -- Emotional
Does the name move people?
Does it create a connection, a feeling, a reaction?
Even a slight emotional response dramatically increases the name's effectiveness.

**Strong example:** Impossible (Foods) -- creates a sense of wonder, defiance,
and ambition.
People react to it.

**Weak example:** "Advanced Protein Systems" -- creates no emotional response.

**Test question:** "Does this name make you feel *anything* -- curiosity,
excitement, warmth, surprise?"

---

## SCRATCH Test -- Red Flags to Eliminate
*Framework by Alexandra Watkins, "Hello, My Name Is Awesome"*

Any name that triggers 2+ of these should be eliminated.
One flag is a caution
worth discussing.

### S -- Spelling-Challenged
Does the name look like a typo, use non-intuitive spelling, or require you to
spell it out loud for people?
Creative misspelling is lazy, not clever.
It
frustrates customers, embarrasses employees, and annoys journalists.

**Red flag examples:** Flickr (where's the E?), Lyft (why not "Lift"?),
Takkion (looks like a misspelling of "tachyon").

**The email test:** Imagine dictating your email address -- name@yourcompany.com --
to someone over the phone.
If you have to say "that's with a Y, not an I" or
"no E at the end," the name fails.

---

### C -- Copycat
Is the name too similar to a competitor or an existing well-known brand?
This
signals lack of originality and opens trademark risk.

**Red flag examples:** A new accounting app called "FreshBooks" when QuickBooks
already exists.
Any AI company adding "-AI" to an existing word format that
other AI companies also use.

**Test:** Search the name against competitors and adjacent categories.
If it
could plausibly be confused with something that already exists, scratch it.

---

### R -- Restrictive
Will the name limit future growth if the company pivots, expands, or enters
new markets?
Functional names are most vulnerable here.

**Red flag examples:** "Burger King" can never credibly sell salads as a core
offering.
"Toys 'R' Us" struggled to expand beyond toys.

**Test:** "If this company doubled in scope or entered an adjacent market in
5 years, would the name still work?"

---

### A -- Annoying
Is the name forced, overly "cutesy," or trying too hard to be clever?
Names
that make people groan rather than smile are brand liabilities.

**Red flag examples:** Excessively punny names for serious products, names that
use "i" or "e" prefixes in 2026, names that substitute numbers for letters
(Gr8, 4Ever).

**Gut test:** "Does this feel like someone was *trying* to be creative, or does
it feel naturally interesting?"

---

### T -- Tame
Is the name flat, descriptive, generic, or boring?
Tame names blend into the
background.
They're the names nobody remembers and nobody talks about.

**Red flag examples:** "National Business Solutions," "Global Tech Services,"
"Smart Home Systems."

**The competitor swap test:** "If a competitor used this exact name, would
anyone even notice the difference?" If the answer is no, the name is too tame.

---

### C -- Curse of Knowledge
Is the name an inside joke, an industry acronym, or a reference that only
insiders understand?
Names need to work for the audience, not the team that
created them.

**Red flag examples:** A database product called "B-Tree" (clear to engineers,
meaningless to buyers), naming a consumer product after an internal code name
that only the team appreciates.

**Test:** "Would my least technical customer understand why this name is
interesting?" If not, it's cursed.

---

### H -- Hard to Pronounce
Does the name fail the "radio test"?
If someone hears the name spoken aloud,
can they spell it?
If they read it, can they pronounce it?
Names derived from
foreign languages the target audience doesn't speak are particularly risky.

**Red flag examples:** Names with ambiguous vowel sounds, names where the
stress pattern is unclear, names that look like they should be pronounced
differently than they are.

**The voicemail test:** "Imagine leaving a voicemail: 'Check out [Name], it's
amazing.' Would the recipient be able to find it online?"
